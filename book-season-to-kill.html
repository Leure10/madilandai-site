The Emperor of Flavor is dead

Augustus Thorne, the critic who could make or break any chef with a single word, collapses at his own glittering New Orleans gala, poisoned by a gift jar of sauce sent by one of the ten disciples he made famous and quietly destroyed.

The police smell cyanide. Nora Vance, a former star chef Gus once ruined, smells something else. Beneath the poison she catches a signature: a flavor with a name and an address. To unmask the killer, she will have to chase that taste around the globe with Sam Rourke, the charming, disaster-prone sommelier who loved her once and never quite got over it.

Chapter 1
The Emperor's Last Mouthful

I broke the first glass of that evening at exactly a quarter to eight, thirty seconds before I saw Nora Vance crossing the dance floor — which, now that I think about it, is pretty much everything you need to know about my relationship with luck.

It was a champagne flute as thin as a diplomatic lie, and it slipped through my fingers just as the jazz band launched into some languid standard, somewhere beneath the heavy chandeliers of the ballroom. It shattered on the 1890 oak parquet with a crystalline sound, almost musical — the kind of sound a sommelier would rather never hear outside a nightmare. A waiter in tails materialized from nowhere with a dustpan, wearing that look that said, without a word: Mr. Rourke is once again contributing to the damage statistics.

Outside, New Orleans was sweating out all its October heat, thick and sweet as syrup, but inside, at Augustus Thorne's annual gala, the air was cold and expensive and smelled of roses, clarified butter, and fear. The chefs' fear, to be precise. Because under that gilded ceiling the whole aristocracy of the world's palate had gathered to flatter the one man who could make or break their careers with a single syllable — and, more importantly, to find out which of them the Emperor intended, that very night, to name his heir.

I'll tell you about Gus Thorne in a moment. For now it's enough to know that he was the kind of man who craved power the way others crave dessert, and that he had, on a mahogany side table beside the podium, a row of jars. Ten jars, like ten little altars. "The offerings," he called them. Every year, his former protégés scattered across the globe would send him, for his gala, a jar of the sauce that had made them famous — a kind of tribute, a kind of prayer. And Gus would taste them one by one, in front of everyone, and pronounce his verdicts. It was his favorite moment of the year. He liked to be fed by the hands of the people he'd brought to their knees.

And that evening, among the hundred perfumed guests, was the woman who had broken my heart seven years earlier and who was, very soon, to set it beating again — though at the time I didn't know that, just as I didn't know that one of those ten innocent jars would be the last thing Gus Thorne ever tasted on this earth.

Nora saw me. She saw the shards at my feet, too. She raised an eyebrow, exactly the way I remembered, and something in me — something I'd believed safely buried under seven years of vineyards and wine lists — flickered back to life with a shiver unbecoming of a man of forty-one.

"Rourke," she said, instead of "good evening." "Still breaking things."

"Only the finest ones," I said. "I have standards."

The corner of her mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but a close relative of one. She wore a dress the color of old wine and, at her throat, that tiny vial of rose vinegar she never went anywhere without. She touched it briefly, brought it under her nose for a second — her way of drawing back the curtain on a room — and sniffed the air, just once.

"Smells like money and grudges," she said quietly. "And something else."

"Like what?"

She looked at me, and for a moment I had the strange feeling she wasn't looking at me but through me, at something about to happen that only she could already sense.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I won't like it."

Up on the podium, Gus Thorne lifted the first little spoon over the first jar, the crowd fell silent with anticipatory pleasure, the saxophone died away, and the great empire of flavor entered, unknowing, its last three minutes of innocence.

Let me step back a moment, though, because you can't understand a man's death without first understanding how badly someone, somewhere, had earned the right to want it.

Augustus Thorne was sixty-six and looked like a cardinal who'd discovered pleasure in place of faith. Broad shoulders wrapped in a bespoke burgundy tuxedo, a silver mane combed straight back, big soft hands belonging to a man who had never scrubbed a pot in his life but had used those hands to break hundreds of careers. They called him "the Emperor of Flavor." His guides — five thick volumes, leather-bound, revised annually with the solemnity of papal bulls — decreed which restaurant in the world deserved to exist and which deserved to vanish. One sentence from Gus could pack a ballroom in Tokyo or send a forty-year-old chef back to his mother in tears, his restaurant mortgaged to the hilt.

But it wasn't the guides that had made him rich. The sauces had. "Thorne's World Table," a line of jars distributed in seventy countries — "authentic" romesco, "heritage" chermoula, tkemali "discovered by Gus Thorne in the heart of the Caucasus." Millions of jars, his face smiling on every label. Except that nothing he sold was his own. Every recipe in that empire had once been the family secret of some poor, brilliant cook whom Gus had "discovered," brought close, wrung out like a lemon, and then discarded. Ten of them, to be exact. Ten protégés, in ten corners of the world. Ten people who had every reason to hate him — and who nonetheless, and this was the brilliant, grotesque part, sent him a jar of sauce every year, like vassals obliged to kiss their lord.

I knew Gus better than I'd have liked. I'd written about him, drunk his wines, covered his galas. I'd watched him humiliate a seventy-year-old Vietnamese chef until the old man kissed his hand, trembling, to thank him for "the lesson." I'd seen him weep with emotion over a plate of pasta and then, that same evening, sign the ruin of the man who'd cooked it, because "talent without discipline is a crime." Gus wasn't evil in the dull way small men are evil. He was evil on a grand scale, with appetite, with relish, with the sincere conviction that the world owed him every mouthful he took from it. I remember one evening in Napa when he put that soft hand on my shoulder and said, red wine glistening on his lips: "Rourke, my boy, the secret is simple. You find a man who loves something more than he loves money. And you take it from him. He'll spend the rest of his life grateful you still exist." I laughed, back then, because I'd been drinking too, and because I hadn't yet understood who he meant.

And seven years ago, one of his guides had carried an assassin of a review that destroyed the one-star restaurant of a young chef named Nora Vance — a review that, to my eternal shame, I had unwittingly fed with a remark tossed off over one glass too many, within earshot of the wrong man, on an evening when I loved her too much to also be careful. But we'll get to that later. Or maybe never, if I'm lucky. Though I've already told you what luck thinks of me.

"Why did you come?" I asked Nora as we both drifted to a spot beside a column, away from the podium.

"Priya Chandra asked me. Gus's lawyer." She took a flute from a passing tray, smelled it, set it back untouched. "Says the old man is 'worried.' That he's been getting letters. Threats. He wanted someone who could 'read' a room."

"And you read the room."

"I read everything, Rourke." She looked at me with a shadow of pity. "Sometimes even people who don't deserve reading."

I'd have had an answer for that. I still do, seven years later — a whole drawer of answers I never gave. I wanted to tell her it hadn't been my fault, which was a lie, or that it had been entirely my fault, which was an exaggeration she'd never have tolerated, because Nora can't stand flattery or martyrdom. So I said nothing, the way I usually do, and watched her watch the room — not as people, but as a platter of ingredients, each with its own smell, its rot or its freshness. I saw her pause, for a second, on a couple of truffle dealers arguing in whispers, on a food blogger retouching her face in her phone screen, on a big cheerful man at a table in the back who was laughing alone at a joke no one had told. I paid him no attention then. I'd regret that for months.

But at that instant Gus Thorne was already tapping his spoon against crystal, calling for quiet, and the room drew in around the podium like iron filings around a magnet.

"Friends," he boomed, in that pipe-organ voice greased with honey. "My children." He swept a wide gesture toward the little table with its ten jars. "Every year, the ones I raised send me their finest work. It's their way of saying 'thank you.'" He smiled, and his teeth were too white. "Tonight I taste them all. And tonight, at last, I tell you to whom I leave everything."

A ripple ran through the room. A hundred chefs, dealers, and culinary parasites leaned forward like a wheat field under wind. Somewhere, a food influencer raised a phone to film, tongue already at his lips.

Beside me, Nora tensed. I felt it in the air, like a change of pressure before a storm. She brought the vial of rose vinegar to her nose again.

"Rourke," she whispered, without moving her lips.

"What is it?"

"Those jars." Her eyes slid across the row of ruby, green, and golden glass, as if counting souls. "Nine of them are old. I can smell it from here — oxidized sauce, lids sitting for months. They were sent long ago."

"And the tenth?"

She was silent a second too long.

"The tenth," she said, "is fresh."

Up under the chandelier, the Emperor of Flavor chose the first jar in the row, unscrewed the lid with a theatrical flourish, dipped his silver spoon, and carried it to his mouth, closing his eyes in pleasure before a room that held, as one man, its breath.
